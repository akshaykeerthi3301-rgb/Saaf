## 1. Name

*Your working name — chosen, said out loud, and sanity-checked.*

Four quick checks before you commit:

- **Say it** — can you say it in a sentence ("I use ___ for that") without stumbling or explaining?
- **Spell it** — could someone spell it correctly after only hearing it?
- **Search it** — is the .com (or a clean variant like get___.com) and the social handle available? Is the name already used by another product in your category?
- **Feel it** — does it fit the worldview, belief, and tone below, or does it fight them?

> Good: a committed working name that passes all four checks, with the domain/handle you'll use noted
> Bad: three candidates you're "still deciding between", a name you have to spell out on every call, a name already ranking in your category

**Your answer:**

**Saaf.** From *saaf saaf batao* — "tell it to me straight" — the idiom every Indian recognises regardless of first language, and a precise description of what the product does, including when the answer is no.

- **Say it** — ✅ "I found it on Saaf." One syllable, no stumble, no explaining.
- **Spell it** — ✅ four letters, phonetically unambiguous.
- **Search it** — ✅ nothing in jobs or careers uses it. Nearest collisions are Saaf Tech (Puducherry, security services) and Saaf Capital (Saudi), both far enough away to be irrelevant. ⚠️ **Registrar lookup not yet run** — category conflict was checked via search, actual domain and handle availability has not been. Do this before any public use.
- **Feel it** — ✅ carries the *refusal* rather than the matching. Every competitor is a job-compound (Jobright, JobTatkal, Job Match Pro, myjobb, Jobmatcher); this is not, and it names the thing that makes the product different.

`[Known drag: "saaf" primarily means "clean", and Indian hygiene and sanitation brands own that association. There is a real chance it reads as a cleaning app before it reads as this. Accepted deliberately — the idiom is stronger than the literal.]`

## 2. Worldview

*What does your brand believe about the world — beyond your product, beyond your category? The conviction you'd still hold if you pivoted the product tomorrow.*

This is the layer above your Contrarian Belief: not what the category gets wrong, but what you believe about how your customers' lives or work *should be*. Patagonia: "the planet can't sustain endless consumption." Basecamp: "work shouldn't be crazy." It's the reason the product exists at all — and it's what your best customers already believe before they ever meet you.

> Good: one conviction about the world, big enough to outlive the product, that your ideal customer would nod at — and some people would argue with
> Bad: a statement about your product or features, "we believe in quality/simplicity/our users", a platitude nobody could disagree with

**Your answer:**

**People can survive a no. What wrecks them is a maybe from a system that already knew the answer.**

Survives a pivot: it would hold unchanged for a college-admissions tool, a loan product, or a visa checker. Rohan would nod — it describes his last eight months of unattributable silence. And plenty would argue: an entire attention industry is built on the maybe, because engagement metrics require it.

Second clause it implies, load-bearing for the honest empty state: **no system should let a person mistake its own coverage gap for a verdict on them.**

## 3. Contrarian Belief

*What does your category get wrong that you get right? The category-specific consequence of your worldview. Frame it as: "Most [category] brands believe [conventional wisdom], but we believe [opposite] — because [reasoning]."*

> Good: specific, defensible, provably true, names the opponent you're rejecting — no competitor could sign their name to it
> Bad: generic, untested, just-different-for-difference's-sake, no reasoning

**Your answer:**

**Most job platforms believe a match score exists to rank everything and show you more. We believe a match score's first job is to refuse — and to publish what it refused and why — because an unverifiable number is exactly how the last eight months got wasted.**

Names the opponent precisely. Jobright's homepage claims "3x interviews landed" and "2,000,000+ users"; publishing a refusal log would contradict the number its conversion depends on. No competitor can sign this without disowning their own front page.

Descends from the worldview: if a system shouldn't hand out a maybe it knows is false, then in this category the score must be willing to say no *and* show its working. The public gate audit is this belief shipped as a feature.

## 4. Tone of Voice

*How does the brand speak — what's the cadence, what words does it use, what words does it avoid?*

Define three things:

- **Voice attributes** — 3-5 adjectives that describe how the brand sounds (strongest in "X but not Y" form: "smart, but not academic").
- **We say / we don't say** — a handful of specific words or phrases the brand uses, and explicit no-go words you'll never use.
- **Example sentence** — one sentence written in your brand's voice that nobody could mistake for another brand.

> Good: specific, distinctive, consistent across every surface (web, app, emails, push notifications, error messages)
> Bad: "professional yet friendly", generic corporate-speak, vague, inconsistent surface-to-surface

**Your answer:**

**Voice attributes**

- **Exact, but not clinical** — every number arrives with its working shown; no dashboard-speak.
- **Plain, but not simplified** — Rohan is an engineer, Meera runs an applicant-tracking system. Never explain down.
- **Certain about method, never about outcome** — say precisely what we did; refuse to promise what happens next.
- **Matter-of-fact about bad news, never apologetic** — no softening a no that is simply a fact.
- **Quiet, but not cold** — no exclamation marks, no cheerleading. Warmth comes from being useful.

**We don't say:** top applicant · perfect match · dream job · unlock · supercharge · 10x · land your dream role · guaranteed · AI-powered *(as a boast)* · we're excited to · unfortunately · just / simply · amazing · **"we think you'd be great at this"** *(an outcome claim wearing a compliment)*.

**Example sentence**

> "We can tell you this posting's three-year floor is your only gap. We can't tell you whether you'll get it, and anyone who says they can is guessing."

Total precision about what the product knows, flat refusal of what it doesn't. Jobright cannot write it — "3x interviews landed" is the guess this sentence calls out.

**Empty-state sentence** — the hardest thing this brand has to say, and where trust is won or lost:

> "3,200 roles indexed today. 47 in your function. 3 cleared your experience band. That's a thin day for our coverage, not a verdict on you."

## 5. Visual Style

*What does the brand look like? Pick a lane and name your references — Step 2 turns this direction (plus a real image you love) into concrete colours, fonts, and tokens.*

**Pick one imagery lane:**

| Lane | When it fits |
| --- | --- |
| **Photography** | The product lives in the real world — real users, real places, real artifacts (Patagonia, Airbnb, Tracksmith) |
| **Illustration** | You want warmth and humanity on a technical product (Notion, Mailchimp, Stripe) |
| **3D / rendered** | The product is the object — devices, spatial, game-adjacent |
| **Flat graphic / type-led** | Bold editorial confidence; the words are the visual (The Economist-shape, brutalist landing pages) |
| **Screenshot-first** | The UI is genuinely beautiful and *is* the story (Linear, Arc, Raycast) |

Then define:

- **Style notes** — 2-3 specifics inside your lane (e.g., "shot on film, available light, real people not models" or "hand-drawn, single-weight line, one accent color").
- **Composition rules** — 2-3 rules for how things are arranged (e.g., "asymmetric grids, generous negative space").
- **References** — 2-3 named brands, films, or artists the look draws from — at least one from outside your product category. One of these references often becomes the image you bring to Step 2.

> Good: one committed lane, specific style notes, named references anyone on the team could look up and reproduce
> Bad: stock photos, "modern and clean", mixing three lanes, references that are all your direct competitors

**Your answer:**

**Lane: screenshot-first.** The hero artifact of this brand is a picture of the product *declining* to show something, so it has to be photographed from the product itself. Jobright is also screenshot-led; the separation is subject matter. Its screenshots sell abundance — dashboards, match counts, two million users. Ours sell subtraction. **Nobody in this category screenshots what they refused.**

**Style notes**

1. **Documents, not dashboards.** Reads like a printed audit sheet, not a SaaS analytics panel. No cards floating over gradients.
2. **Arithmetic visible.** `3,200 → 47 → 3` is the signature graphic device. Always show the subtraction, never only the total.
3. **No gradient, no glow, no orb, no glassmorphism.** Flat fills, real borders, high contrast. The 2026 category default is disqualified by our own belief — a product that publishes its refusals shouldn't look like it's hiding behind a blur.

**Composition rules**

1. **Side-by-side is the hero format** — the portal's claim left, Saaf's withholding plus reason right. One recurring composition, used everywhere.
2. **The refused role is drawn, not hidden.** Withholding is a visible state with a reason attached, never an absence. If refusal is the product, refusal needs a design.
3. **Type-led, product second.** Sentences carry the argument; screenshots evidence it. Never a hero image with no words.

**References**

- **Zerodha / Varsity** — adjacent, Indian, and the proof that calm, dense, unaspirational design beats persuasion in this market. No advertising, no aspirational imagery, no promises of quick wealth.
- **Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters** *(outside category)* — the refusal to decorate *as* the credibility signal. Plain text, no imagery, trusted precisely because it isn't selling.
- **The packaged-food nutrition label** *(outside category)* — standardised, tabular, legally honest, universally trusted. This is the form the public gate audit should take, and the most likely image to carry into Step 2.

`[Dependency: screenshot-first needs a UI worth screenshotting, and no code exists yet. Until the gate runs this brand has no imagery — one more reason the refusal screenshot is the first thing to build.]`
