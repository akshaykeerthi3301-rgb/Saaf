# Saaf

*A daily shortlist of roles you can actually get, ranked by honest odds.*

**Last updated:** September 2026

> Named **Saaf** in the Design phase, September 2026 — from *saaf saaf batao*, "tell it to me straight." The repository directory is still `jobmatch`; that's a path, not the brand. Full rationale in `productos/design/1-Product-Identity.md`.

---

## Summary

**Saaf** is a free daily job shortlist for Indian professionals in their first five years of work — the ones applying to 30–50 roles a quarter and hearing nothing back. It pulls openings from a curated registry of Indian companies' own hiring boards, and before anything gets scored it removes every role the candidate is structurally disqualified from: the posting that wants fifteen years of experience when they have two, the one that requires a chartered accountancy qualification they don't hold, the one that asks for a paid-media budget they've never been given. What survives is ranked by honest odds, and every role arrives with the single line explaining the number — *"72% — go-to-market and stakeholder management match; the job description's three-year floor is your one gap."* When a role is worth the forty minutes, Saaf will draft a résumé and cover letter tailored to that specific posting — but only when asked, and only by reordering, trimming and re-wording facts already in the profile the candidate confirmed; a fabrication guard blocks any generated document that makes a claim the profile doesn't support, and the remedy is to add the fact, never to let the model assert it. It stops there: this is a pre-application tool, not an auto-apply bot, and it answers one question — *which of these deserves my next forty minutes?* It is free permanently, with no card, no ads and no paid tier, and it is built as a portfolio artifact held to the standards of a real product.

## Customer

All five people this product serves are the same person in five functions: employed, one to four years into a career they've outgrown, searching at night on a personal device because the work laptop is monitored and the current employer must not find out, sitting on a ₹4–8 lakh annual pay gap, receiving confidently wrong match signals and unattributable silence from the portals, and willing to pay exactly ₹0. All five rate what they use today 3 or 4 out of 10 — they keep using it because there's no alternative, not because it works. What differs between them is the disqualifier that quietly ends their applications, and that difference is the whole product.

**Rohan (engineering)** is the design lead: 24, two years at Infosys on a banking client's maintenance project in Bengaluru, ₹7.5 lakh after this year's 6% hike, wanting a product-company engineering role at ₹12–17 lakh. Weeknights 10pm to midnight with the laptop on the bed, longer and more hopeful bursts on Sunday afternoons — hopeful at 10pm, deflated by 11. He applies in Sunday bursts of fifteen to twenty and then nothing for a fortnight, asks batchmates for referrals over WhatsApp (his highest-yield channel and the one he feels worst about using), refreshes his Naukri profile weekly because he heard it lifts him in recruiter search and has no idea whether that's true, and hoards thirty-plus tabs he closes unread. His gate is years of experience, increasingly with a data-structures screen attached.

**Priya (consulting)** is two years into a Big-4 or services consulting seat at ₹9–12 lakh, wanting strategy or business operations at a funded startup at ₹12–18 lakh, searching from a hotel room on travel weeks — more privacy than Rohan, far less time. Her experience reads generic on paper: every job description wants "ownership of a business metric" and her résumé says "supported the workstream." Her gate is institute tier — *"Tier-1 MBA preferred"* — which she fails invisibly and then blames herself for, which makes naming it out loud the most valuable thing we can do for her. **Karthik (marketing)** runs paid campaigns at an agency or direct-to-consumer brand at ₹4–8 lakh, wanting growth at a funded software company at ₹8–15 lakh, and is the most comfortable of the five searching semi-openly because marketing people are expected to be visible. Postings list channels as binary — *"must have run ₹50 lakh+ in paid spend," "must have owned search-engine optimisation end to end"* — and he's run three of the five asked for with nobody telling him which of the missing two are fatal and which are learnable on the job. His gate is channel ownership and budget scale, frequently not years at all.

**Meera (HR)** is a recruiter or HR generalist at around ₹5.5 lakh wanting a people-partner role at ₹9.5 lakh, and she searches in stolen minutes during the workday — she's inside a hiring tool all day, so the tab is less conspicuous — while being the most exposed of the five, since her own HR team would know instantly. She knows exactly why she's being filtered and can do nothing about it, because she operates the applicant-tracking system doing the filtering; her gate is years plus a recruitment-versus-generalist split that postings treat as non-transferable. That makes her the harshest critic of any matching claim and, if convinced, the best-networked evangelist available — she ranks fourth on design priority and is the most valuable beta user we could have. **Arjun (finance)** is a chartered accountant two years post-qualification in audit at ₹7–10 lakh, wanting business finance at a funded startup at ₹10–17 lakh, on a rhythm unlike the other four: his search stops dead for two weeks every month-end and quarter-end. Roughly 40–60% of mid-level Indian finance roles are filled through referrals before they're ever posted, so he distrusts every list he's handed — including ours on day one. His gate is a literal credential — chartered accountant, chartered financial analyst, or company secretary — binary in a way years of experience never are, and the cleanest demonstration of the whole approach.

They're ranked by design priority, not importance — five personas with equal weight is the same as none, because you can't build one onboarding screen for five people. **Not for:** the eight-plus-year services professional making the same switch, or the final-year student chasing internships — the registry barely carries their roles, so their lists come back thin. We don't turn them away. Gating them out would reproduce the exact injury this product exists to fix, so a thin list carries the honest arithmetic instead — *"3,200 roles indexed today · 47 in your function · 3 cleared your experience band"* — with the shortfall attributed to our coverage, never to the candidate.

## Problem

The signal these people get isn't missing — it's confidently wrong. India's major portals rank candidates against roles they are structurally disqualified from: a two-year candidate presented as a **"Top Applicant"** on a posting that asks for fifteen. Because the match arrives with confidence attached, the candidate spends the forty minutes, tailors the résumé, applies — and is filtered at the first automated screen for a disqualifier that was printed in the job description all along. Repeat that across 30–50 applications a quarter and the result is months spent on roles that were never winnable, zero callbacks, and no way to attribute the silence: was it the résumé, the fit, the timing, the market? Eight months in, Rohan has changed nothing, because he doesn't know what to change. A candidate on Blind — Bangalore, thirteen-plus years, fifty-plus applications across LinkedIn, Naukri and Instahyre — reduced the whole category to four words: *"not even one call."* The search starts with the appraisal letter, and 2026 was the leanest cycle since 2020 — TCS 4.5–7%, Infosys 6–8%, Wipro 7–9%; a 6% hike on ₹7.5 lakh is about ₹45,000 a year, roughly ₹3,750 a month before tax. That fires annually. The sharper trigger fires monthly: a batchmate posting on LinkedIn about joining a product company. The cost is a ₹5–8 lakh annual pay gap left unclosed, four to six hours a week of unproductive searching, an application that eats 30–40 minutes on a night that only has 45 in it, and a compounding penalty nobody puts a number on — every additional year in services makes the switch harder, because product companies discount services experience. What they do instead is Sunday bursts of one-click applications, referral asks over WhatsApp, and LeetCode as insurance; they've done it for eight months and rate it 3 out of 10. And the failure runs both ways: roughly 80% of applications for Indian roles arrive through LinkedIn and roughly 95% are irrelevant to the posting, so recruiters stop reading and callbacks collapse even for the candidates who genuinely fit.

## Mechanism

Four layers, and three of them run before any language model sees the job. **First, supply.** Rather than scraping portals, the product maintains its own registry of Indian companies' job-board tokens across the six applicant-tracking systems those companies already use — Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workable, Recruitee and SmartRecruiters — each of which publishes open, zero-authentication endpoints intended to be read. It's seeded at about a hundred boards by hand, then extended by automated discovery. A live probe of six boards on 2 September 2026 returned 233 roles, of which 137 were in India, 90 were in India and not senior-titled, and 66 were in India, junior and outside engineering — so roughly 300 boards is on the order of 3,000 addressable roles, refreshed daily and legally clean. **Then, normalisation.** The candidate's profile and every job description resolve into one shared vocabulary of skills and occupations through ESCO, the European Commission's open occupation taxonomy, and O\*NET, its US counterpart — which is what lets a single engine serve every corporate function without hand-writing a taxonomy per role family. Profiles are built parse-then-confirm: a résumé is parsed to pre-fill the fields, the candidate confirms every one of them before the first match runs, and the uploaded file is deleted the moment parsing finishes. **Then, gates before scores** — plain deterministic code, deliberately not AI. Hard requirements are evaluated as filters rather than weighted features, and the *type* of requirement differs by function: an experience band in engineering, a credential in finance and law, channel and budget scale in marketing, institute tier in consulting, a recruitment-versus-generalist split in HR, and location and work authorisation throughout. A disqualifying role cannot surface however well its skills score, with one labelled exception — a Stretch tier capped at a two-year gap, which applies only where the gate is experience-typed, because a credential you don't hold cannot be stretched. **Finally, explanation**, the only place a model is used at all: it writes the one-line reason a role scored what it did and, on request, drafts the tailored résumé and cover letter. It never decides eligibility. Calls are routed across providers, so a model being deprecated is a configuration change rather than a rebuild. The whole thing runs daily with memory — the list is what's new since yesterday, not a reshuffle of the same roles — and it promises no volume, because a short honest list beats a padded one. That is why this works where the portals don't: the "Top Applicant on a fifteen-year role" failure is structurally impossible here, and it's impossible *precisely because* this layer is rules rather than a model. A language model asked to judge eligibility weighs when it should gate. That's how the bug is born in the first place.

## Why

The advantage compounds in the one place a competitor can't copy quickly and a foundation-model release can't absorb: the registry. Every board added is permanent supply, it grows weekly, and nobody else has it — which is exactly why the gating logic gets open-sourced as public proof while the registry stays private. The category makes the gap easy to see. Jobright charges $39.99 a month for the strongest match scoring in the market and indexes US roles only. Teal runs free to $29 a month and is a tracker and résumé builder with no matching engine behind it. Simplify is free to $39.99 with unlimited autofill and a 4.9-star rating across 3,800 reviews, but it fills applications rather than deciding which are worth filling. The auto-apply tools at $99–249 a month are the category's reputational failure mode — they are a large part of why recruiters stopped reading in the first place. Nobody is serving Indian supply, legally, at this precision, at ₹0.

The legal position is an advantage rather than a constraint we tolerate. *hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn* established that scraping public data isn't a computer-fraud violation, but hiQ still lost on breach of contract and settled with a $500,000 judgment and a permanent injunction — so LinkedIn and Naukri are excluded from the supply chain entirely, and any competitor built on those surfaces carries a risk this product doesn't. Everything except the explanation layer survives the next model release: a one-prompt ChatGPT user cannot maintain three hundred boards, cannot run unattended every morning, cannot hold a profile's state across months, and will reproduce the fifteen-year bug exactly, because a model weighs where this product gates.

The risk taken off the table isn't money — the product is free — it's the candidate's time, credibility and confidentiality, in three commitments enforced in code rather than asserted in copy. *Nothing you're disqualified from:* a disqualifying role reaching the main list is a defect, not a judgement call, and every recommendation carries a one-tap "this shouldn't be here" that gets triaged as a bug. *Nothing invented in your documents:* generated résumés and cover letters may only reorder, trim and re-word facts from the confirmed profile, and the fabrication guard blocks any output making a claim the profile doesn't support. *Nothing shared about your search:* the uploaded file is deleted once parsed, no employer is ever contacted, nothing is public or sold, no recruiter can find you here, and deleting the account takes one click and no email address. Each commitment costs the seller something real — a public defect surface, engineering that will sometimes refuse output a user wants, and deliberately never accumulating the résumé corpus that would otherwise be the most valuable asset in the building. That cost is the test of whether a guarantee is real. What the product does not yet have is any distribution advantage at all: no audience, no channel, and five personas with no single channel that reaches them. That's an open problem, not a moat.

## Business model

Nobody pays. The product is free permanently — no card, no ads, no paid tier hiding behind a feature you already use — and that's a positioning decision, not deferred monetisation. It's a portfolio artifact built to real-product standards, and the value ratio is the acquisition engine: a ₹5–8 lakh annual problem addressed at ₹0. The customer's own anchors explain why free is the right number rather than a cop-out. Rohan spends ₹0–5,000 direct on his search today and rates everything that costs money poorly: Naukri's FastForward résumé writing is ₹4,200 at mid-level and ₹5,300 senior, with express delivery adding ₹1,000–2,000; Coding Ninjas runs ₹50,000 to ₹1.5 lakh; Scaler Academy ₹2.5–4.5 lakh, often on deferred or income-share terms; LeetCode Premium about ₹2,800 a year; Instahyre and Cutshort are free. He has never paid a subscription for job search, and the category has taught him that paid job tools are either expensive or unreliable. There is a documented ceiling of roughly ₹200–300 a month, from a personal discretionary budget competing directly against a Scaler instalment — but it applies only *after* demonstrated results, and it's recorded as an unexercised option, not a plan. Free is sustainable rather than subsidised because cost-to-serve is engineered to near zero: Vercel's Hobby tier for hosting, Supabase or Neon for the database, GitHub Actions for the daily run (more generous than Vercel's two-job cron cap on Hobby), Groq at roughly 1,000 requests a day or Gemini at roughly 1,500 for the explanation layer, Resend for up to 3,000 emails a month, and the MIT-licensed docx-js for document generation. One constraint follows from that and shapes everything downstream: Vercel's Hobby plan is non-commercial only, and Vercel counts *advertising* a paid product as commercial — so introducing a paid tier, or even announcing one, requires moving to Pro first.

## Proof

What proves the market exists: people already pay real money for weaker versions of this. Jobright charges $39.99 a month — raised 33% from $29.99 — for the strongest match scoring in the category, and indexes US roles only. Teal runs free to $29 as a tracker and résumé builder. Simplify is free to $39.99 and holds 4.9 stars across 3,800 ratings for filling applications rather than choosing which are worth filling. Auto-apply services charge $99–249. In India the same demand surfaces as ₹4,200 for a Naukri résumé rewrite and ₹2.5–4.5 lakh for a Scaler cohort. And the pain is documented verbatim rather than inferred: Blind threads titled *"LinkedIn feels dead in India, where are people actually getting callbacks"* and *"50 days zero interview calls, is the job market broken"* — the latter from a Bangalore candidate with thirteen-plus years and fifty-plus applications across all three portals who got *"not even one call"* — while r/developersIndia's community wiki carries a canonical page titled *"Service Based to Product Based."* A wiki page maintained for this exact transition is the clearest evidence the customer is real.

What proves this product specifically: nothing yet, and nothing is claimed. As of September 2026 no code has been written, no user has seen it, the Mini-Launch has not been run, and there is no launch log, no believers list, and no first-party signal of any kind. The five personas are research-backed and interview-unvalidated — zero interviews conducted, ten planned at two per persona. Proof here is entirely future-tense, and this should be the first section of this document to change.

What we're testing next, in the order it becomes earnable. **The refusal screenshot**, available the day the gate works: a major portal presenting a two-year candidate as "Top Applicant" for a role requiring fifteen, side by side with the same profile in this product, that role withheld and the reason stated. Verifiable by anyone in thirty seconds, reproducible on demand, and the entire thesis in one image. **The public gate audit** at launch: a live page reporting roles ingested, roles gated out, and the reason distribution — how many for experience, how many for qualification, how many for location. No competitor publishes this. **The open-sourced gate**, so a sceptical reader can read the code that makes the fifteen-year failure impossible instead of taking our word for it, alongside the registry count, published and dated, compounding weekly. **The first cohort** at roughly sixty days post-launch: 25–50 candidates from the named group reporting the share of shown roles they judged themselves eligible for — target 100% — plus applications sent, callbacks, and three to five named testimonials. The Mini-Launch should ship once the refusal screenshot exists; a post carrying that one image will land far harder than one describing it, and its bar is a single reply.

## Goal

What we're tracking near-term is a single rung on the signal ladder — reply → conversation → signup → activated user → payment — and we are below the bottom of it. Nothing has been launched, so the immediate bar is the first rung: build the gate far enough to produce the refusal screenshot, ship the Mini-Launch carrying that image, and get one reply from a real person. One reply is the win at this stage. A stranger asking *"can I try it?"* is the rung above, and that's what the Design-phase relaunch aims at.

Two operational numbers tell us whether the thing itself works. The **registry count** — about a hundred boards seeded by hand, three hundred as the near-term target, published and dated so the growth is checkable rather than asserted. And the **eligibility rate**: the share of roles shown to a candidate that the candidate judges themselves genuinely eligible for, target 100%, measured first on the launch cohort. That second number is the only one that directly tests the promise, and unlike a match score it can't be gamed by the product grading its own homework.

What would make us change course. The ladder's own rule comes first: the same rung missed twice is a pivot conversation, not a messaging tweak. Beyond that, three findings would force a rethink. If the ten interviews say the night-time, personal-device, employed-and-hiding pattern is an artefact of designing from one case, the persona set needs rebuilding before more product decisions rest on it. If the honest empty state merely disappoints the poorly served segments more politely rather than retaining them, that position needs a different expression — though not a reversal to signup gates, which would reintroduce the original harm. And if Arjun reports that under half of his real opportunities ever reached a job board, the product should say so plainly in its own interface rather than quietly serve him a partial market.
